# Scientific Writing Polish
 
Revise text to comply with the project's writing rules, drawn from *Writing in the Sciences* (Kristin Sainani), the Concise & Compelling Writing reference sheet, and the Guidelines for Writing an Academic Document.
 
The job is **sentence- and paragraph-level polish**, not structural rewriting. The author keeps their voice; the skill removes what gets in the way of it.
 
## Operating principles
 
These govern every edit. When a rule below would conflict with one of these, these win.
 
- **Light touch.** Make the minimum change that fixes a real rule violation. If a sentence already works, leave it alone. Do not edit for the sake of editing.
- **Preserve the author's voice.** Keep their word choices, register, and rhythm. The output should read like the author on a good day — not like a different writer.
- **Never change meaning.** Do not alter claims, findings, numbers, citations, technical terms, or hedging ("may", "suggests"). If a fix would risk the meaning, leave the text and move on.
- **Polish, don't restructure.** You may merge or split sentences and reorder within a paragraph for flow. Do not add or cut ideas, or reorganize the document.
- **Output only the revised text.** No preamble, no summary of changes, no commentary. If the user later asks what changed, then explain — but not before.
## Workflow
 
1. **Read the whole passage first.** Understand what it is saying before touching a word.
2. **Detect the context.** If the passage is a methods description (a recipe of steps performed), passive voice is acceptable where the actor is unimportant — do not force it active. Everywhere else (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, general prose), prefer active voice.
3. **Run the revision passes below**, in order. Earlier passes (clutter, verbs) often dissolve problems that later passes would otherwise flag.
4. **Reread the result against the operating principles.** Did meaning survive? Does it still sound like the author? Did you change only what was broken?
5. **Output the clean revised text and nothing else.**

## Pass 1 — Cut the clutter
 
Clutter is any word that adds length without adding meaning. The reader's attention is finite.
 
**Dead-weight phrases — delete on sight:**
"as it is well known", "as has been shown", "it can be regarded that", "it should be emphasized that", "it is important to note that", "the basic tenets of", "in order to" (→ "to"), "the fact that".
 
**Replace long phrases with short ones:**
 
| Long | Short |
|---|---|
| a majority of | most |
| a number of | many |
| are of the same opinion | agree |
| less frequently occurring | rare |
| all three of them | the three |
| give rise to | cause |
| due to the fact that | because |
| have an effect on | affect |
| based on the assumption that | if |
| in the event that | if |
| at this point in time | now |
 
**Eliminate negations** — direct phrasing is faster to read:
 
| Negation | Direct |
|---|---|
| not honest | dishonest |
| not harmful | safe |
| not important | unimportant |
| does not have | lacks |
| did not remember | forgot |
| did not pay attention to | ignored |
| did not succeed | failed |
 
**Remove "there is / there are"** when a real subject can lead:
"There are many physicists who like to write." → "Many physicists like to write."
 
**Cut weak adverbs:** very, really, quite, basically, generally, actually, simply, clearly — unless they carry genuine meaning. Often the adverb signals a weak verb that should be made stronger instead (see Pass 2).
 
**Cut unnecessary jargon and invented acronyms.** Keep only standard, widely understood acronyms (DNA, RNA). If an acronym appears once or twice, spell it out and drop it.

## Pass 2 — Strengthen the verbs
 
Verbs make the sentence move. Weak writing hides the action in nouns or filler.
 
**Prefer active voice.** Active = subject, verb, object. It is shorter and names who did what.
- Passive: "My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me."
- Active: "I will always remember my first visit to Boston."
- Exception: methods passages, where the actor is genuinely unimportant. "The samples were centrifuged" is fine.
- "We" and "I" are acceptable and support active voice. Do not strip them in the name of objectivity.
**Minimize "to be" verbs** (is, are, was, were, be, been, am). They are inert. When a stronger verb is hiding nearby, use it.
 
**Un-bury nominalized verbs** — turn the noun back into a verb:
 
| Noun phrase | Verb |
|---|---|
| make a decision | decide |
| provide a review of | review |
| offer a confirmation of | confirm |
| take an assessment of | assess |
| provide a description of | describe |
| obtain estimates of | estimate |
| shows a peak | peaks |
| has seen an expansion in | expands |
 
**Choose a verb that absorbs the adverb.** "reports approximately" → "estimates"; "estimates that ... in the future" → "projects".
 
**Keep the subject and main verb close, and near the start of the sentence.** Do not bury the verb behind a long subordinate clause.
- Buried: "One study of 930 adults with multiple sclerosis receiving care in two settings found that..."
- Fixed: "One study found that, of 930 adults with multiple sclerosis receiving care in two settings, ..."

## Pass 3 — Fix the punctuation
 
| Mark | Use it for | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| **Comma** | items in a list; joining two full sentences with a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so, yet, for, nor, or); setting off non-essential elements and introductory phrases | separate subject from verb; separate verb from object; join two independent clauses on its own ("I went home, I was tired" → use a period, semicolon, or comma + conjunction) |
| **Semicolon** | joining two closely related independent clauses with no conjunction; separating list items that contain internal commas | join a dependent clause to an independent one |
| **Colon** | after a *complete* clause, to introduce a list, explanation, or amplification; joining two clauses when the second extends the first | follow an incomplete clause ("You need: patience, skill" — wrong; the colon must follow a full clause) |
| **Em dash —** | like parentheses, but for information you want to emphasize rather than tuck away; a strong interruption | overuse; reserve it for emphasis |
| **Parentheses** | genuinely non-essential information; introducing an acronym. The sentence must remain complete and correct if the parenthetical is removed | carry information the sentence needs |
 
Use punctuation variety to control flow — but never insert a mark the sentence does not need.

## Pass 4 — Enforce parallelism
 
Matched ideas take matched grammatical form. The reader predicts the pattern and reads faster.
 
- **Paired clauses** (joined by and, or, but): match the structure on both sides. "The velocity decreased by 50% but the pressure decreased by only 10%."
- **Lists:** every item takes the same form — all verbs, or all nouns, or all infinitives. Do not mix.
  - Parallel: "The program aims to identify cases, track outcomes, and reduce costs."
  - Broken: "The program aims to identify cases, tracking outcomes, and cost reduction."
- This applies to numbered and bulleted lists too. Reading the items aloud exposes a broken pattern.
---
 
## Pass 5 — Tighten paragraphs
 
- **One idea per paragraph.** If the text drifts to a second point, that is a new paragraph. Splitting is allowed; merging unrelated ideas is not.
- **Lead with the punchline.** Speech builds to its conclusion; writing inverts that — point first, then supporting detail, then a strong closing sentence. The reader should know the paragraph's point from sentence one.
- **Make it flow, in this order of priority:** (1) the logic of the ideas — a natural sequence flows on its own and costs no words; (2) parallel structure across sentences; (3) transition words (however, therefore, in addition) — useful but easy to overuse, so prefer simple ones and use them sparingly. Overused transitions usually paper over weak logic.
- **Keep paragraphs short** — roughly three to five sentences. Dense blocks discourage reading.
- Do not use "however" merely to signal a topic shift; it must mark a genuine contrast.
---
 
## Pass 6 — Grammar checks
 
- **"Data" is plural:** "the data show", "the data are".
- **affect vs effect:** affect is the verb, effect is the noun (in normal usage).
- **"compared with"** for comparing similar things in scientific writing (not "compared to").
- **that vs which:** "that" introduces an essential clause (no comma); "which" introduces a non-essential clause (with commas).
- **Verb tense:** past tense for completed actions ("we found that..."); present tense for things still true ("Figure 1 shows...", "the data suggest...").
---
 
## A note on repetition
 
Do **not** swap a repeated word for a synonym automatically. First ask whether the repeated word is needed at all — if not, cut it. If it *is* needed, repeating it is usually better than reaching for an awkward synonym. In scientific writing, key terms (study groups, variables, instruments, disease names, models) must stay consistent: changing the word makes the reader suspect you mean a different thing. Avoid "elegant variation" — synonyms used only to dodge repetition.

## Worked examples
 
**Sentence — clutter + nominalization:**
Before: "It should be emphasized that the algorithm provides a reduction of the processing time due to the fact that it makes use of caching."
After: "The algorithm reduces processing time because it caches results."
 
**Sentence — passive to active:**
Before: "A significant difference was found between the two groups by the researchers."
After: "The researchers found a significant difference between the two groups."
 
**Sentence — negation + weak verb:**
Before: "The results were not inconsistent with our hypothesis, and there are several factors that could be the explanation."
After: "The results were consistent with our hypothesis, and several factors could explain them."
 
**Paragraph — punchline first, flow, parallelism:**
Before: "We collected samples from three sites. The samples were analyzed using chromatography. The northern site had elevated readings. It seems that contamination is present at the northern site, which is the main thing we found."
After: "Contamination is concentrated at the northern site. We collected samples from three sites and analyzed them by chromatography; only the northern site showed elevated readings."
 
In each case the meaning, the findings, and the author's terminology are unchanged — only the clutter, weak verbs, and ordering are fixed.

## Output
 
Match the input's shape: a revised sentence for a sentence, a revised paragraph for a paragraph. Add no headers, no "Here is the revised version", no list of changes.
 
- **If you made edits:** return only the revised text — nothing else.
- **If the text already complies** and needs no changes: do not invent edits. Return the text unchanged, followed by one short line on its own:
  `No changes needed — already complies.`
  This single line is the only commentary the skill ever volunteers; use it solely for the no-change case, never alongside an actual revision.
If the user later asks what changed or why, explain then — but not before.
